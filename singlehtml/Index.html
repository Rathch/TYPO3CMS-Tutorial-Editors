TYPO3 Editors Guide 

Once you have installed TYPO3 using the TYPO3 - Getting Started Tutorial; the next step is to log in to the backend of the CMS and start adding pages and create some content. In the TYPO3 world we often call users who carry out these tasks "Editors".

This guide contains detailed information about all of the common tasks an Editor is likely to perform including granting other users access to the backend, creating pages, adding content to pages in the form of content elements and uploading and managing various file types including images and PDFs.

This guide assumes that you are already familiar with TYPO3. If this is not the case, visit the TYPO3 Concepts to get started.

All of the examples used in this guide are taken from a TYPO3 installation running the Official Introduction Package.

Logging in to TYPO3 backend 

If you are a TYPO3 editor, the backend is where you will most likely spend most of your time. So let's start by logging in.

Navigate to https://example.org/typo3 in your browser. The /typo3 part can be changed, so if /typo3 does not work, check your project documentation or ask your administrator for the correct backend URL.

If you are having problems logging in, have a look at the troubleshooting tips.

  • Troubleshooting: Forgotten password
  • Troubleshooting: You cannot log in
  • Troubleshooting: Advice for administrators
  • Ending your session

Troubleshooting: Forgotten password 

  1. Click on Forgot your password?

    Go to the backend login page and select Forgot your password?.

  2. Enter your email address

    Use the same email address that you entered during registration.

  3. Open the email and follow the link

    If you haven't received the email, check your spam folder and double-check that the email address you entered is correct.

  4. Enter a new password

    Click on the password recovery link in the email and then enter a new password. Make sure you choose a password that fits your administrator's rules.

Things your administrator can check if this is not working correctly:

  • the password reset feature is enabled
  • your backend account has a valid email address associated with it
  • the TYPO3 installation can send emails.

Troubleshooting: You cannot log in 

Things you can check are:

  • JavaScript is enabled in your browser
  • your credentials are correct and valid
  • whether your IP address has changed (you will automatically be logged out).

The backend is locked for maintenance 

Your TYPO3 installation may be locked for maintenance and a message will appear indicating that TYPO3 is in maintenance mode. Check with your administrator.

Too many failed logins 

If you enter the wrong login credentials too many times, you may be locked out of the backend. You will see a message such as "The login is locked until 2026-08-31 10:58 due to too many failed login attempts from your IP address."

Wait till the time is up and then try logging in again.

The login box reloads but no error message is displayed 

Login cookies could be incorrectly set. Try deleting your browser cookies.

Troubleshooting: Advice for administrators 

See TYPO3 Getting Started, Troubleshooting common TYPO3 backend login problems.

Ending your session 

When you have finished updating content or managing records, you should properly end your session to secure your workspace. TYPO3 keeps your session open as long as you are actively working, but explicitly logging out ensures that unauthorized users cannot access your backend account from your device.

How to log out 

To log out of the TYPO3 backend, have a look at the top bar:

  1. Click your name in the top-right corner.
  2. Select Logout from the menu.

You will be logged out straight away and taken back to the login page.

Creating pages 

Pages form the core structure of your website. Here you will learn how to create new pages on your site using the page tree. New pages are invisible in the frontend by default, so you will also learn how to make your pages visible.

There are two ways to create a new page. You can either drag and drop from icons above the page tree or right click on the tree to bring up a context menu. In both methods you select an existing page in the tree and your new page is created underneath.

If you look at the icons above the page tree, you will see that you can create different types of pages. Here we will just create a standard web page.

First open the Content > Layout module in the left sidebar. The page tree is in the middle. Click around the page tree to see the selected page displayed in the right pane.

Method 1: Using drag and drop 

  1. Open the page creator: Click the + Page button at the top left of the page tree, or find the row of icons representing different types of pages.

  2. Position your page:

    • If using + Page: Click on a location for your new page in the popup window. Select the page with the globe icon ("root" page) if you want to create another main page or select an existing page to create a "child" page. Then click on Insert subpage. Choose the standard page type.
    • If dragging an icon: Grab the Standard Page icon and drag it into the page tree. You will see the same popup window.
  3. Name your page: Enter a title in the popup window.

Method 2: Using the context menu 

  1. Choose your location: right click on the "root" page (with the globe icon) to create another main page. Right click on an existing page if you want to create a "child" page.

  2. Select New subpage from the context menu.

  3. Enter the page title in the popup window.

Making your new page visible in the frontend 

New pages are hidden from the public by default. There are 3 ways that you can make your pages visible:

  • In the page tree: Right-click on the page and toggle the Enable switch from the context menu.
  • In the page properties of a page: Click on the page, click on Edit page properties at the top of the main pane, click on the Access tab, and toggle the Page visible switch (see below).
  • You can also publish while you are creating a page: Scroll to the bottom of the popup window and toggle the Enable switch.

Creating multiple pages 

If you need to build out a new section of your website quickly, you can create many pages at once from the page tree.

First click into the Content > Layout module in the left sidebar.

  1. Open the bulk creator: Right-click a "parent" page in the page tree and choose More options > Create multiple pages from the context menu.

  2. Name your pages: Enter a title and a page type for each page. Here we are just creating normal "standard" pages.

  3. Expand your list: Click Add more lines to add five more pages as needed.

  4. Set page position and visibility if necessary at the bottom of the screen:

    • Positioning: Toggle Place new pages after the existing subpages to add the pages below any existing subpages. Leave it off to place them above.
    • Visibility: Toggle Hide new pages or Hide new pages in menus.
  5. Save your changes: Click Create pages.

Tip

Hidden pages are completely offline and inaccessible to visitors. Hidden in menus pages are online but will not appear in your site navigation. Visitors will still be able to visit your pages via URL.

Working with pages 

You can organize pages in your website using the page tree.

First click on Content > Layout in the backend sidebar to display the page tree in the middle pane.

Moving pages 

You can change your site hierarchy or reorder pages by dragging-and-dropping inside the page tree.

  1. Locate the page you want to move within the page tree.

  2. Click and drag the page to its new position. A horizontal line will appear to preview where the page will land.

  3. To nest a page (making it a "child" of an existing page), drag it directly over the target page until a gray outlined background appears.

  4. Release your mouse. A confirmation message will prompt you to choose between copying or moving.
  5. Select Move this item.

Tip

To copy a page instead of moving it, follow the exact same steps but select Copy from the confirmation prompt.

Deleting pages 

To remove a page entirely, click and drag the page to the right. You will see the Delete action.

Context menu actions 

Right click on any page in the page tree to bring up the context menu and see the many editing shortcuts and page functions.

Page types 

Each item in the page tree has a distinct page type.

Default page types 

TYPO3 includes several page types out of the box.

Pages 

Standard
The default building block for standard web pages.
Backend User Section
A restricted page type that is only displayed in the frontend if a user is logged into the TYPO3 backend.

Links & redirects 

Shortcut
Creates a redirection to another page in your page tree. Frontend visitors are forwarded to the other page.
Mount Point
Similar to a Shortcut, but instead of redirecting to another page, it redirects to a whole subtree somewhere else in the page tree. This is particularly useful for frontend menus and means you don't have to duplicate lots of pages in your page tree.
Link
Similar to a Shortcut, but this page type can forward users to external URLs, individual content elements/page sections, downloadable assets (such as files), system folders, email forms, and data from extensions.

Special containers 

Folder
A simple container used to store records (data from extensions) or pages. Containers are hidden in the frontend. Records are usually stored in containers and then referenced via plugins on other pages. Pages that are not included in the main menu are usually stored in containers (for example, a footer menu container)
Menu Separator
A separator element that can be used as a visual separator in the backend page tree. Menu separators can also be referenced in TypoScript (the TYPO3 frontend configuration language) to add lines/spaces to navigation menus in the frontend.

Page title / alternative navigation title 

The Page Title is used as label in menus and in the <title> tag in the HTML header. It is also used to generate the URL Segment.

When you specify an Alternative Navigation Title this title is used as label in the menus while other usages of the "Page Title" stay unchanged.

The "Subtitle" is only used if your site package configured it to be output somewhere.

This tab is used for search engine optimization. It uses the cs_seo system extension. See section SEO Tab in chapter Search engine optimization (SEO) for TYPO3 editors for more information.

Social media 

The fields on this tab are used to enrich social media snippets for the URL of the page. See section Social media tab in chapter Search engine optimization (SEO) for TYPO3 editors for more information.

Metadata 

The fields available on this tab depend on how your site is configured. How the data is used by the frontend depends on TypoScript which depends on your site configuration.

Typically, you might see the Abstract field, and editorial details like Author Name and Last Update.

Appearance 

This tab contains properties that influence how the page is rendered. By default it contains settings for the page layout, which influences the pages appearance in both the frontend and the Page Module in the backend. Sometimes a site package defines additional fields in this tab regarding appearance. Refer to the documentation of your site package or ask your integrator.

Page layout / Backend Layout 

Backend Layouts influence the general structure of a page not only in the backend but also in the frontend. A Backend Layout influences which content areas are available in the Page Module to Manage content in them. A Backend Layout is usually bound to its own frontend template which influences the appearance in the frontend.

Behaviour 

In this tab there is usually not much to do for an editor. Advanced editors can use it to exclude certain pages from the search.

Resources 

This tab lets you link media files to the current page. How those files are handled depends on your frontend rendering configuration.

Access 

This tab lets you control the visibility of the page.

For more information, see Elements visibility.

Categories 

How categories for pages are used during rendering is up to the site package. By default they do not do anything.

Notes 

Use this tab for your own editorial notes and internal comments, such as reminders or to-do lists.

Troubleshooting content element edits 

Content element has no edit button 

If you have no permissions to change a content element, you will see that element in the overview but no edit, hide or delete button appears.

Possible reasons:

  • The content element may only be edited by a user or group that created it.
  • Only admins may edit this content element.
  • The content element is of a type that your group may not edit.

An administrator can support you in finding out why you cannot edit this content element.

Content element does not get saved 

If one of the fields you edited contains an illegal value now or if you did not fill out a required field, you cannot save your changes.

If the field you changed is on a different then the currently changed tab, you might not notice it right away.

Error message after saving a content element 

Such error messages can happen if you are editing content during or right after an update. Note the time and exact error message and copy the URL from your browser where you saw the error. Send these to your integrator or programmer.

Changes not visible in the frontend after saving a content element 

There can be various reasons:

  • Your browser cached the old content: Delete your Browser cache.
  • Try Previewing the page without clearing the cache.
  • Your changes did not get saved due to an error (see possible errors above).
  • Your site is using Workspaces.
  • Try Manual cache clearing if you have the permissions to do so.
  • If problems persist, contact your administrator.

Non-breaking spaces and soft hyphens 

Two characters that are normally invisible are highlighted with a colour in the rich text editor so that you can see where they have been placed:

  • A non-breaking space keeps two words together on the same line, so they are never split across a line break.
  • A soft hyphen marks a point where a word may be hyphenated and wrapped to the next line, but only if the word does not otherwise fit.

The colour highlighting is only shown while you edit. It helps you spot these characters in the editor and does not appear on the website itself.

You can insert both characters with a keyboard command.

You can also upload files from your computer straight into the field. TYPO3 uploads them and adds them to the field. Only files with one of the allowed file extensions are uploaded.

  • Drop files onto the field: Drag the files from the file manager of your computer over the content element. A drop zone covers each file field, so you can drop the files anywhere onto the field.
  • Paste files into the field: Copy the files in the file manager of your computer, or copy image data such as a screenshot. Click into the field and press Ctrl + V. On a Mac, press Cmd + V. If you paste without clicking into a field first and the form shows several file fields, TYPO3 asks which field to add the files to.

Browse the file tree, then select a folder. The file selector always starts in the root folder of the default storage. Use the search field to find a file by name. The search includes all the subfolders of the selected folder.

The file selector can upload files from your computer to the folder it shows:

  • Click Select & upload files in the section Upload files below the files of the folder.
  • Drop the files anywhere onto the files of the folder.
  • Copy the files in the file manager of your computer, or copy image data such as a screenshot, and press Ctrl + V. On a Mac, press Cmd + V.

Only files with one of the allowed file extensions of the field are uploaded. If a file with the same name already exists in the folder, TYPO3 asks what to do with it. The folder then shows the uploaded files, so you can select them right away.

Translation strategies 

When you translate content, there are two modes: a connected mode and an independent mode.

Translate (connected) 

Use this mode if your site requires a strict, parallel content structure across all languages. TYPO3 maintains a direct link between the source element and its translation.

  • When the source text changes, TYPO3 marks the translation as out of date.
  • While you are translating, you can see the changes in the source language next to your text.
  • Translation teams can be notified and can track what still needs review.

Copy (independent) 

Use this mode if your localized pages require unique layouts, different content structures, or completely independent text.

  • Decoupled content: TYPO3 creates a standalone copy of the source content in the target language.
  • Structural freedom: No link is maintained, allowing the localized page structure to diverge entirely from the source.

Search engine optimization (SEO) for TYPO3 editors 

Welcome to our small SEO introduction. We will explain the basic fields the TYPO3 core provides and introduce the SEO tools this way.

All the following fields are part of the page record.

You can reach it by:

  • Open the Content > Page module
  • Use the pen (Edit page properties) in the top bar icon to edit the page record.

An alternative way is to use the context menu.

  • Open Content > Page module
  • Right click the page you want to edit in the page tree
  • Select the Edit action

General tab 

Page title 

The page title field is used for several purposes. From a SEO perspective it is mainly used to define the default URL segment and as a fallback title, if you do not set a specific SEO title. More information about the usage of the title fields can be found at Title for search engines.

URL segment (slug) 

The URL segment is the part of the URL the user enters to access your page. Depending on your use case URLs have to fulfill certain criteria.

By default the URL segment is based on the page tree and page title, but you can override this and set it yourself.

If you want to optimize your page for search engines, it is recommended to have at least the keyword you want to be found on in your URL segment. Please make sure to check other best practices with your SEO specialist.

SEO tab 

Title for search engines 

The Title for search engines field is used for the <title>...</title> tag of your web page. This title is used in the search results of search engines. There are some best practices for the value of this field.

  • Use a speaking title, which targets your audience
  • If possible, use the keywords you want to be found on in the title
  • Please make sure your title fits in a Google Snippet. You can use third party extensions to help you with this.

When no value for the Title for search engines field is defined, it will have a fallback to the field Page title. It will show the page title in the search results in that case.

The title can have the name of the site as a prefix or suffix. The behaviour of this can be defined by an integrator.

By default the title will have the name of the site as a suffix, separated by a dash.

Description 

The description field is important for SEO purposes. This field is used to instruct search engines to use this text to show as a teaser of your page in the search result snippets. With this description, you can give the user a clear overview of the topic of this page.

Hint

It is best practice to use at least the keywords you want to be found on in this description. Make sure the description is not too long, so it fits in the search result snippets.

See also

You can use the "Missing Meta Description" widget to get an overview of all pages without a description and edit them directly.

Index this page 

Defines whether the page should be indexed by a search engine or not. Disabling indexing also removes the page from the XML sitemap.

Follow this page 

Defines whether search engines should follow the links on this page.

Canonical link 

You want to avoid duplicate content because your ranking can suffer from this. If you have duplicate content, you have to link to the origin of the content. This can be an internal page or an external page. You can use the default link wizard of TYPO3 to select the destination. If you don't set a canonical link yourself, TYPO3 will generate a link to the current page.

Hint

Avoid duplicate content warnings by supplying the original source of this content.

Change frequency 

This field is used in the XML sitemap and will give search engines a hint how often the content of your page is likely to change.

For example: News in an archive are “never” updated, while your home page might get “weekly” updates.

Hint

Please be aware that this just gives a hint to search engines and search engines can also ignore this information if they have better information regarding the change frequency.

Accessibility for editors 

People with disabilities can use a page when they can perceive the content, operate it, and understand it. The W3C Web Accessibility Initiative publishes the guidelines that describe those requirements. You publish the words, images, media, and forms. Several of the failures that show up most often on the web sit in exactly that content.

What the figures say about your part of the work 

Two public scans measure how often pages fail checks that a program can run. A program cannot see every barrier. The W3C states that no tool alone can decide whether a site meets the guidelines. The figures below are the failures the scans could count. They are a floor, and they already show where an editor changes the result.

One million home pages 

In February 2026, WebAIM checked the home pages of the top 1,000,000 sites and published the results in The WebAIM Million. 95.9% of those home pages had at least one automatically detected failure of the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2. The average home page had 56.1 such errors. 96% of all detected errors fell into six types. The same six types have led the list for seven years. WebAIM writes that addressing these few types would significantly improve accessibility across the web.

Failure Home pages Where you can change it in TYPO3
Low contrast text 83.9% The colors usually come from the theme. If body text is hard to read, ask your integrator. If you set a text color yourself, that color is yours to correct.
Missing alternative text for images 53.1% You write it, on the file and again for the place where the image is used.
Missing form input labels 51% You write the label when you build the form.
Empty links 46.3% You write the link text. A linked image with no alternative text is an empty link too.
Empty buttons 30.6% Buttons in the theme are the integrator's. A control you add needs a name a reader can follow.

The report gives a closer count for the items you type in:

  • 16.2% of all images had no alternative text. 10.8% of the images that did have alternative text used a file name, a filler such as "image", or the same text as the image beside them. More than one in four images had one of these problems. 45% of the images with no alternative text were links, so the link had no usable name. One in four linked images had no alternative text.
  • 15.2% of home pages had ambiguous link text such as "click here", "more", or "continue". Those pages had 5.3 such links on average.
  • Skipped heading levels, such as a heading of level 2 followed by a heading of level 4, appeared on 41.8% of home pages. 18.1% had more than one heading of level 1.

Home pages that the same study classified as TYPO3 CMS had 38.5 detected errors on average, compared with 56.1 for all home pages. WebAIM notes that a higher or lower count next to a technology cannot always be attributed to that technology. A lower average does not make the content accessible. The alternative text, the headings, and the link text are still written per page.

A second scan of page content 

AudioEye's accessibility statistics report a separate scan of about 400,000 pages across about 15,000 sites. In that scan the average page had 297 accessibility issues, and 2% of the sites passed 70% of the WCAG criteria the scan could test. AudioEye is a vendor. Treat the article as that company's reading of its own scan, and use the WebAIM report when you want a method you can read in full.

On images and links, the AudioEye article reports:

  • 38% of images had missing alternative text, and 60% of images had no alternative text. 93% of domains had at least one page with an inaccessible image.
  • 64% of pages had links that were not clear to people with disabilities, about five such links on a page. 80% of sites had at least one page with an inaccessible link.
  • In AudioEye's 2023 index, cited in the same article, 1 in 4 forms were missing descriptive labels.

The article lists both image figures. It does not define how "missing alternative text" differs from "no alternative text", so this manual keeps them as two reported figures.

Read together, the two scans point at the same editorial work. Alternative text, link text, headings, and form labels are fields you fill in. Contrast of the theme, empty buttons in the template, and the language of the whole page are set elsewhere. Ask your integrator about those.

Legal requirements 

This page is not legal advice. The law that applies depends on the country, on whether the organization is a public body, and on what the site sells or provides. Ask the person responsible for the site which standard you must meet. The W3C states the same limit on its own list of laws: it does not make regulations, the list is not complete, and the entries are not legal advice.

The guidelines laws point to 

The technical requirements usually point at the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1, or at a later edition. WCAG 2.1 is a W3C Recommendation. It is written as testable statements, independent of a particular technology. Content that conforms to WCAG 2.1 also conforms to WCAG 2.0. The W3C encourages the use of the most current version when a policy is written or updated. WCAG 2.2 is that later Recommendation.

WCAG groups the requirements into four principles. The wording below is a short reading of those principles for an editor. The normative text is the Recommendation.

  • Perceivable. People can take in the information. Text alternatives, captions, and contrast belong here.
  • Operable. People can use the page. Headings, page titles, and link text belong here, as does keyboard access, which is mostly the template.
  • Understandable. People can follow the page. The language, the labels, and the error messages belong here.
  • Robust. Assistive technologies can read the page. That is mostly the template and the code.

Each requirement has a level: A, AA, or AAA. Your organization names the level that applies to the site. The W3C list of web accessibility laws and policies shows which WCAG edition each listed policy refers to.

European Union 

Two European directives cover a large part of what an editor publishes. Each member state writes them into national law, so the national text is the one that binds the organization.

Directive (EU) 2016/2102 is the directive on the accessibility of the websites and mobile applications of public sector bodies. The W3C policy list records it as an accessibility law for the public sector, based on WCAG 2.1.

Directive (EU) 2019/882, the European Accessibility Act, sets accessibility requirements for specified products and services. Member states had to adopt the national measures by 28 June 2022 and apply them from 28 June 2025. The EUR-Lex summary of the Act lists what is covered. For an editor, the relevant services include e-commerce, consumer banking, e-books, and parts of passenger transport such as websites, mobile services, and electronic tickets. The Act also covers products such as computers, smartphones, e-readers, and certain self-service terminals.

The Act does not cover every website. The summary names these exclusions, among others:

  • Microenterprises that provide services.
  • Pre-recorded video and office files published before 28 June 2025.
  • Content of a website or app that is an archive, meaning it is not updated or edited after 28 June 2025.
  • Third-party content that the organization does not fund, develop, or control.

The Act itself is a list of accessibility requirements for those products and services. It is not a sentence that says "use this WCAG version". The version and the level come from the national law and from the standard that law names. Ask which ones apply to your site.

Other countries 

Outside that European frame, and for the national text inside it, use the W3C list of web accessibility laws and policies. The list has a last-updated date on each country. It includes, among many others, non-discrimination laws and public-sector rules in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and Switzerland. If your country is missing, or the entry looks old, the national authority is the source to follow. The W3C asks to be told about additions and corrections.

Writing accessible text 

The W3C collects the writing practices on Writing for Web Accessibility. The sections below follow that page and name the TYPO3 field for each practice. The examples are illustrations of the wording. They are not records from a particular site.

Provide informative, unique page titles 

Success Criterion 2.4.2 requires a title that describes the topic or purpose of the page. A person using a screen reader hears the title first. The same title is the label of the browser tab, so a row of tabs stays usable when several pages of the site are open.

Set the Page title in the page properties. If the page has a Title for search engines, that value is the document title. When it is empty, TYPO3 uses the page title. By default the site name is added as a suffix, separated by a dash. An integrator can change that pattern. Ask when you need the title to follow a fixed order.

Put the part that distinguishes this page first. The W3C's own examples use a page name, then the organization, and they include the step when a page is one step of a process.

Situation Title
A page whose title is only the organization Camino
The page name first, then the organization Pilgrim stories - Camino
One step of a process Book a bed (step 1 of 3) - Camino

"Camino" on its own fits the home page. It does not tell anyone which inner page is open. "Page 3" and "News" have the same problem when the site has many news pages: add the topic, for example "Route closed in March - News".

Use headings to convey meaning and structure 

Success Criterion 2.4.6 asks for headings that describe the topic. Screen reader users jump from heading to heading. A heading that exists only because it looks larger is missing from that list, and a heading that was styled to look small is still announced as a heading. The W3C tutorial on headings shows a heading outline.

Almost every content element can have a heading. When your permissions allow it, set the level under Headlines > Type. Pick the level from the outline of the page. A subsection sits one level under the section it belongs to.

The rich text editor can insert headings too. Use those for the structure inside the text. Use the content element heading for the title of the element. Two headings with the same wording, one on the element and one inside the text, give the reader the same entry twice.

In the WebAIM Million report of February 2026, skipped heading levels were present on 41.8% of home pages, and 18.1% had more than one level-1 heading. How your site turns the page title into a heading depends on the site package. Ask your integrator whether the page title is already the level-1 heading. If it is, the first content element heading is level 2.

Example. A page "Pilgrim stories" contains one story and a practical note:

  • Level 1: Pilgrim stories. This may already be the page title.
  • Level 2: The night at the mountain hut.
  • Level 3: How to get there.
  • Level 2: What to pack.

A level-4 heading directly under the page title leaves a gap. The size of the type is a design decision. If a level-2 heading looks too large, ask your integrator to adjust the stylesheet. Changing the stored level to level 4 would break the outline.

Make link text meaningful 

Success Criterion 2.4.4 requires that the purpose of a link can be determined from the link text, together with its context. People who list all the links on a page hear the link text alone. "Click here" and "Read more" then become a list of links that all sound the same. In the February 2026 WebAIM report, 15.2% of home pages had link text of that kind.

When you create a link in the rich text editor, write the words that say where the link goes, select those words, and then open the link dialog. If you insert a link without selecting text, TYPO3 uses the title of the link, or the title of the target. Read that inserted text. A page title can be a good link name. A file name such as scan-final-v3.pdf is a poor one.

The W3C contrasts a link that says "click here" with a sentence that names the destination. The same pattern:

  • Weak: For the route, click here.
  • Clear: Read the route over the pass.

When the target is a file, include the type and, if you know it, the size. The W3C example is "Proposal Documents (RTF, 20MB)". A TYPO3 equivalent is "Route description (PDF, 1 MB)".

A heading of a content element can itself be the link. The heading text is then the link text, so the heading has to name the destination.

A linked image uses the alternative text as its accessible name when the image has no other text. In the WebAIM report, 45% of images with no alternative text were links. Write the alternative text as the name of the destination: "Route description (PDF)", for a linked cover image.

Write meaningful text alternatives for images 

Success Criterion 1.1.1 requires a text alternative for non-text content. The W3C's writing guide puts it this way: the alternative text provides the information or the function of the image. A decorative image needs no alternative text. The W3C tutorial on images separates informative, decorative, and functional images. A short practical guide is Alt-texts: The Ultimate Guide, from Axess Lab.

In the Media module, edit the file metadata and fill in the alternative text there. That text is the default wherever the file is used. When you place the image in a content element, you can override the alternative text for that one use. A portrait in the media library might have the alternative text "Portrait of the hut warden". On a page about winter opening hours, the same file might need "Hut warden at the counter where winter hours are posted".

The W3C shows the difference with a photo of someone charging a phone. The weak alternative text is "Charging phone". The useful one describes the step the photo is there to teach: "Plug cable into the bottom edge of the phone." The same test, applied to a route page:

  • Weak: hut.jpg
  • Weak: image
  • Useful, when the photo carries the information: Wooden signpost for the mountain hut, 2 hours.
  • Empty, when the photo only decorates a paragraph that already says all of this.

Describe the information. Skip "image of" and "photo of". The screen reader already announces that it is an image.

An alternative text is usually not visible on the page. The Description is a different field. Themes often show the description under the image, where everyone can read it. The alternative text replaces the image for someone who cannot see it. The description adds something for everyone. They should not be copies of each other when the description is visible, or the reader hears the same sentence twice.

For a decorative image, leave the alternative text empty so assistive technologies can skip it. If your installation will not store an empty value, ask your integrator how decorative images are marked.

Text that matters belongs in the text field. Success Criterion 1.4.5 requires real text when the same visual result can be achieved with text. A heading saved as a picture, or a price saved as a picture, cannot be resized as text and has no alternative unless you write one. A logo is an allowed exception. Its alternative text is the name in the logo.

Provide transcripts and captions 

The W3C writing guide asks for a transcript of audio-only content, such as a podcast, and captions for video that has a soundtrack. The transcript and the captions include the spoken words and the sounds that carry meaning. The W3C examples of those sounds are "door creaks" and, in a video transcript, a description of an action such as someone leaving the room. The requirements are Audio-only and video-only (1.2.1) and Captions, prerecorded (1.2.2).

You can place the transcript in the text of a Text & Media element, next to the video. Give it a heading, for example "Transcript", so it can be found from the heading list.

Example, a short clip in which a warden speaks and a door closes:

Warden: The pass is closed until Friday.
[The hut door closes.]
Warden: Use the valley path.
Copied!

How captions are attached depends on the player your site uses. The core manual for editors does not document a captions field on the media element. Ask your integrator which file to upload, or which field the player reads. A transcript in the text still helps when the captions file is missing, and it helps people who cannot play the video.

Success Criterion 1.4.2 requires a way to pause or stop audio that plays automatically for more than three seconds, or a volume control independent of the system volume. Leave Autoplay off unless you have that control. The setting is described in Configure the video.

Provide clear instructions 

Success Criterion 3.3.2 requires labels or instructions when the content asks the user for input. The W3C example of an instruction that states the rule is: "Password should be at least six characters with at least one number (0-9)." An error should say what is wrong. The W3C examples are "The username 'superbear' is already in use." and "The password needs to include at least one number."

When you build a form in the Forms module, the label is the accessible name of the field. "Name" is weaker than "Your name" only in tone. "Field 1" is a failure. Placeholder text inside the field disappears while the person types, so it does not replace the label.

State the format next to the field when a reader cannot guess it:

  • Date of arrival (DD.MM.YYYY)
  • Phone number, including the country code

If the form shows an error, write the message so it names the field and the change. "Check your input" leaves the reader to try every field. "Date of arrival uses the form DD.MM.YYYY" names the fix.

Keep content clear and concise 

The W3C recommends short sentences, familiar words, abbreviations expanded on first use, and lists where a list is the structure of the information. The criteria behind that advice include Unusual words (3.1.3), Abbreviations (3.1.4), and Reading level (3.1.5). Reading level is level AAA. Clear sentences still help at every level. The W3C page links onward to guidance for people with cognitive and learning disabilities, under the heading "Use clear and understandable content".

Expand an abbreviation the first time you use it: "Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)". A second mention can be "WCAG" alone.

Example. A sentence written to sound official:

"Utilization of the contact form is mandatory for the submission of all inquiries pertaining to group visits."

The same instruction:

"To ask about a group visit, use the contact form."

A list is easier to scan than a sentence of options. "You can get there by bus, by the valley path, or by booking the hut taxi" can be three items when each option needs its own detail.

Success Criterion 1.4.1 says that color is not the only means of conveying the information. "Fields in red are required" excludes a reader who cannot see the red. Add the word "required" to the label.

Success Criterion 1.3.1 requires the structure to be in the markup. In the rich text editor, use the list buttons for a list, and mark the header row when you insert a table. A bold first row that is not a header is only bold. The W3C tutorial on tables shows header cells.

Tips and tricks 

Use this list on a page before you publish it. Each item names the requirement it supports. The explanations of those requirements are linked from Legal requirements. How to fill the fields is in Writing accessible text.

A program can find some of these failures. It cannot decide that the page is accessible. The W3C says so in its introduction to evaluating accessibility, and it keeps a list of evaluation tools submitted by the providers. The W3C does not endorse the products on that list. For a first pass a person can do without a product, use the W3C Easy Checks.

Before you publish 

Read the title out of context 

Open the page and read only the browser title. It should name this page. "Home", the organization name alone, or "Page" fails that test on an inner page. Set the page title, and the title for search engines when you use that field, as described under Provide informative, unique page titles.

Walk the headings 

List the headings in order, including the levels. You should get an outline of the page. A level jumps from 2 to 4 when a level was chosen because it looked smaller. One topic keeps one heading. The page title may already be the level-1 heading. Ask your integrator if you are unsure, then start the content element headings at level 2.

Read the links as a list 

Collect every link on the page and read the texts without the sentences around them. Each text should say where that link goes.

Change these when you find them:

  • "Click here", "here", "more", "read more", "continue".
  • A raw address as the only link text, when the page has a title you can use instead.
  • An empty link, including an icon or an image with no alternative text.
  • Two links with the same text that go to different places. "Download" twice, for two different files, needs the file name in the link.

A file link names the type: "Hut rules (PDF)".

Cover the image 

Cover the image with your hand and read the alternative text. If you still learn what the image was there to say, the text is doing its job. If the paragraph already says it, the image is decorative and the alternative text should be empty.

Reject "image", "photo", "icon", a file name, and a text identical to the caption that is already visible under the image. The Description and the alternative text are different fields. The description is often shown to everyone. The alternative text stands in for the image.

Play the video with the sound off 

If you cannot follow a video with the sound off, the captions or the transcript are missing. Put a transcript in the text of the content element and give it a heading. Ask your integrator how the player on your site takes a captions file.

Leave automatic playback off. If a clip must start by itself and it lasts more than three seconds, the visitor needs a way to pause it or to change its volume separately from the device.

Check color and text saved as an image 

"Required fields are shown in red" needs the word "required" as well. A heading that is a picture, or a phone number that is a picture, should be typed as text. A logo may stay an image. Its alternative text is the words in the logo.

If you colored text in the editor, check the contrast. Success Criterion 1.4.3 requires a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1. Large-scale text, at least 18 point or 14 point bold, needs at least 3:1. Text that is pure decoration, inactive, or part of a logo has no contrast requirement under that criterion. The colors of the theme are the integrator's. Low contrast was the most common failure in the February 2026 WebAIM report: 83.9% of home pages, with 34 instances on an average home page.

Label every field 

Read the form with the placeholders ignored, because placeholders vanish while someone types. Every field needs a visible label. Say the format when it is easy to get wrong: "Date of arrival (DD.MM.YYYY)".

Trigger an error on purpose. The message should name the field and the change. "Invalid input" does not. The W3C example of a message that names the problem is "The password needs to include at least one number."

Mark a language change 

The language of the page is part of the site setup. An administrator sets it. When you edit a translation, you are already in that language. See Working with languages.

A quotation or a title in another language, inside a page of one language, needs to be marked. Success Criterion 3.1.2 is the requirement. If your rich text editor has a language option, set it on that phrase. If it has none, ask your integrator to enable it. Until then, avoid dropping in unmarked passages in a second language.

Mark the header cells of a table 

A table of data needs header cells, not a bold first row. A screen reader can then repeat the header as the reader moves across a row. The W3C tutorial on tables shows the patterns, including tables with two headers. A layout that only uses a table to place two pieces of text side by side is a question for your integrator. Prefer two paragraphs or a list when the content is not a table.

What to hand to your integrator 

These failures showed up often in the February 2026 WebAIM report, and you cannot fix them in a content element:

  • Body text, buttons, and form fields in the theme whose contrast is too low. 83.9% of home pages had low-contrast text.
  • Buttons and icons in the template that have no accessible name. Empty buttons were on 30.6% of home pages.
  • The language of the document, when it is missing. 13.5% of home pages had no document language.
  • A skip link, keyboard focus, and the order of the keyboard tab. Those belong to the template.
  • A captions control, if the media player has nowhere for you to add a captions file.

Send a specific page address and a specific element. "The body text on the home page is gray on white" gives your integrator something to change. "The site is not accessible" does not.

Cache 

Computing a page to be displayed in the frontend requires time and resources.

TYPO3 (and many other CMS) use caching to help reduce the amount of time and resources it takes to generate a page.

When you display a page in the frontend, TYPO3 first checks if that page can be found in the cache and has already been calculated. If the page is found in the cache, the previously calculated result is delivered right away. If the page is not found in the cache, it is generated by collecting data from different sources and performing some calculations. The result is then delivered to the visitor and stored for future use.

Browser cache 

The browser on the client machine (Chrome, Edge, Firefox, etc) also caches pages. Sometimes you cannot see changes you have made to a page because your browser has cached the old content.

It depends on the browser and the operating system, how you can clear your browsers cache.

On Windows in most browsers you can press Ctrl + F5 to clear the browser cache.

Automatic cache clearing 

With TYPO3, the cache of a page is cleared automatically in the following cases:

  • You changed something about the page itself like its title
  • You changed a content of the page like a text or image on that page
  • In certain time intervals, usually once a day
  • If content with a starttime or endtime set becomes visible or invisible
  • In some other cases that your integrator or developer might have defined

Scenario: Changes are not visible in the frontend 

If the cache did not get automatically cleared, the changes you made might not be visible right away in the frontend.

Some scenarios in which manual cache clearing might be necessary:

  • You changed the title of a page. You view the affected page and everything looks fine. Now you go to the start page and cannot see your change in the menu. The following happened: Changing a pages title deletes the cache of the affected page. It does, however, not delete the cache of other pages like the start page.
  • You added a news record. It does not appear in the news list. Creating a record does not automatically delete the cache of any page unless the integrator made some configurations.
  • You changed content on one page that should also be displayed on other pages. This is often done with static parts of the page that should be editable such as the footer, a carousel in the header, etc.

Manual cache clearing 

Clearing the cache of a single page can be done without ill effect in most installations.

To clear the cache of that page, go to the module Content > Layout and click the button representing a lightning bolt with the tooltip Clear cache for this page.

When you reload the affected page, the changes should be visible right away. If the changes are not visible, clear the browser cache. Or use an incognito tab in your browser.

Only editors who have the relevant user rights can flush the cache of all pages. To do so, click the lightning bolt button in the top bar and choose Flush frontend caches. If you cannot see the button, you might not have the rights to carry out this action:

Warning

After flushing the cache each page has to be generated a new

on the first request for that page. This requires resources on the server such as processor and hard disc usage. If too many pages have to be generated at the same time, generating them might take longer (maybe minutes instead of seconds) or fail all together. Therefore some TYPO3 installations do not allow manual cache flushing. Talk to you administrator about the preferred strategy here.

Previewing a page without clearing the cache 

If you need to preview a page and cannot clear its cache you can use the parameter no_cache=1 to preview the page as it will look once the cache is cleared. This option might have been disabled by the administrator however.

For example, visit https://example.org/community/events?no_cache=1.

File abstraction layer (FAL) 

TYPO3 has a file abstraction layer (FAL) integrated which abstracts technical details (where the file is stored) and allows to work with different storage forms.

FAL also allows the editors to manage meta data that can be displayed wherever an asset is displayed. The metadata can also be used for copyright notices.

Copyright and data privacy 

Copyright 

Whenever you use files not created by yourself, for example images or videos, the copyright must be considered.

Using a file or even a text without proper copyright lead to high fines. If in doubt, do not use a content without consulting legal advice first.

Even images that you created yourself might infringe someones copyright. For example you may not take pictures of certain artwork or draw a picture of your favorite cartoon character and publish it.

Have a look at the related Wikipedia article: Copyright

Data privacy 

In different countries there are different laws about data privacy. In general, you may not publish pictures of or data from any person alive or dead unless the person consented. There are some exceptions for persons of public interest such as politicians, scientists or movie stars.

Personal data does not only include a persons name but each information that might help to identify the person in question such as phone number, email address, IP address, nick names, pictures, car license plates etc.

Wikipedia has a good article on Personal Data which explains the situation in different countries.

Publishing personal information without consent might lead to high fines in some countries. If in doubt, do not use any personal information without consulting legal advice first.
